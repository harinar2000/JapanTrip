Reading the train signs

  • Match the destination board.Trains show their final stop, not the next one. Each step lists a “Toward” cue. Match it, or ride the train that stops next at the stations I name.
  • Local, Rapid, Express.Faster trains skip small stations. If your stop is small (Inari, Fukakusa, Tofukuji), take a Local and check Google Maps.
  • Station codes.Signs show a letter and number, such as G09 for Ginza on the Ginza Line. The number counts stations, so a bigger number means farther along the line.
  • Exits matter.Big stations have dozens. Google Maps shows the exit. Follow the yellow signs, and use elevators with bags.
  • Pay with an IC card.Suica or PASMO works on Tokyo, Kyoto, Osaka and Hiroshima transit, but not for the Shinkansen or highway bus tickets, which you already hold or book separately.
  • Avoid 07:30 to 09:00 with bags.Commuter trains at that hour are packed. Your bags are only on trains on Nov 24 and Nov 28, so leave early or take a taxi for those two mornings.

Eating as two vegetarians and two meat eaters

  • Say it first.“Bejitarian desu. Niku, sakana, dashi nuki de onegaishimasu.” (I’m vegetarian. No meat, fish or fish stock, please.) Show the Japanese on your phone if the shop is busy.
  • Dashi hides everywhere.Miso soup, udon broth, stews and many sauces use fish stock (dashi) or bonito flakes (katsuobushi). Ask even for “vegetable” dishes. Okonomiyaki and takoyaki are topped with bonito flakes; say “katsuobushi nuki.”
  • Pork hides in plain sight.Gyoza fillings, curry roux, fried rice and ramen toppings often include pork or lard. “Vegetable” curry can still be made with animal-based roux. Ask once, nod, and trust the answer.
  • Good mixed-group formats.Okonomiyaki, ramen shops with a vegan bowl, Indian curry, pizza and shojin ryori all work because each person can order differently at one table.
  • Cash is still king.Many small ramen shops and temples take only cash. Keep ¥10,000 to ¥20,000 on you.
